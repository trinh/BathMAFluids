Theorem 3.3.2 Forces in inviscid fluids.

In the case of inviscid fluids, we assume that the surface force exerted on (interior) volume elements is accounted for solely by a pressure, \(p\text{,}\) which acts in the inward normal direction at each point, with
\begin{equation*} F_n \bn = -p \bn. \end{equation*}
🔗
Consequently, the surface force, given by (3.3.4), is
\begin{equation} \iint_{\partial V} (-p) \bn \, \de{S} = \iiint_V (-\nabla p) \, \de{V}.\tag{3.3.5} \end{equation}
🔗
In particular, for the case of inviscid fluids, we ignore tangential internal forces.
🔗
🔗

Proof.
The divergence theorem in its usual form applies to the flux of a vector field, \(\iint_{\partial V} \boldsymbol{F} \cdot \bn \, \de{S} = \iiint_V \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{F} \, \de{V}\text{,}\) whereas the left-hand side of (3.3.5) is a vector. We therefore take its dot product with an arbitrary constant vector \(\boldsymbol{c}\text{,}\) which reduces the problem to scalars.
🔗
Since \(\boldsymbol{c}\) is constant, it may be moved inside the integral, giving
\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{c} \cdot \iint_{\partial V} (-p) \bn \, \de{S} = \iint_{\partial V} (-p \boldsymbol{c}) \cdot \bn \, \de{S}. \end{equation*}
Now apply the divergence theorem with \(\boldsymbol{F} = -p\boldsymbol{c}\text{.}\) Again using that \(\boldsymbol{c}\) is constant, \(\nabla \cdot (-p \boldsymbol{c}) = -\boldsymbol{c} \cdot \nabla p\text{,}\) and hence
\begin{equation*} \iint_{\partial V} (-p \boldsymbol{c}) \cdot \bn \, \de{S} = \iiint_V (-\boldsymbol{c} \cdot \nabla p) \, \de{V} = \boldsymbol{c} \cdot \iiint_V (-\nabla p) \, \de{V}. \end{equation*}
Combining the two results,
\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{c} \cdot \left[ \iint_{\partial V} (-p) \bn \, \de{S} - \iiint_V (-\nabla p) \, \de{V} \right] = 0. \end{equation*}
🔗
This holds for every constant vector \(\boldsymbol{c}\text{.}\) Choosing \(\boldsymbol{c} = \be_1, \be_2, \be_3\) in turn shows that each component of the bracketed vector is zero, so the vector itself is zero, which is (3.3.5).
