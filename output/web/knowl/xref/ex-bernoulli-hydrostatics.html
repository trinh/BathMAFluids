Example 3.5.12 Hydrostatics and Pascal’s bursting barrel.

For a fluid at rest, \(\bu = \mathbf{0}\text{.}\) The flow is then trivially steady and irrotational, so Theorem 3.5.8 gives
\begin{equation*} \frac{p}{\rho} + gz = \textrm{constant everywhere}. \end{equation*}
If the free surface is at \(z = H\text{,}\) where \(p = p_\text{atm}\text{,}\) then
\begin{equation*} p = p_\text{atm} + \rho g (H - z). \end{equation*}
The pressure at depth \(d = H - z\) is \(p_\text{atm} + \rho g d\text{.}\) It depends only on depth, not on the shape of the container or the amount of fluid above.
🔗
This explains Pascal’s bursting barrel. A long thin vertical tube is attached to a sealed barrel full of water, and the tube is then filled. Although the column in the tube holds very little water, it raises the pressure throughout the barrel by \(\rho g h\text{.}\) For a \(10\) m column, that is about \(10^5\) Pa, roughly one atmosphere. Acting on a lid of diameter \(0.5\) m, this is a force of about \(2 \times 10^4\) N, the weight of roughly two tonnes.
