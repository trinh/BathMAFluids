    2. Leibniz’s rule.

    Reynolds’ Transport Theorem Theorem 3.1.3, which relates to the passage of a time derivative through an integral, is a general form of the 1D Leibniz rule:
    \begin{align} \dd{}{t} & \int_{a(t)}^{b(t)} f(x, t) \, \de{x} \notag\\ & = \int_{a(t)}^{b(t)} \pd{f}{t} \, \de{x} + f(b(t), t) \dot{b}(t) - f(a(t), t)\dot{a}(t).\tag{3.6.1} \end{align}
    🔗

    (a)

    Examine the figure in Figure 3.5. Can you associate the quantities in Leibniz’s rule with the circled elements in the figure?
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Derive Leibniz’s rule (3.6.1). You may assume that \(f\) and \(\pd{f}{t}\) are continuous, and that \(a(t)\) and \(b(t)\) are differentiable.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Let \(I(t) = \int_{a(t)}^{b(t)} f(x, t) \, \de{x}\) and start from the definition of the derivative,
    \begin{equation*} \dd{I}{t} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{I(t + \Delta t) - I(t)}{\Delta t}. \end{equation*}
    Write \(I(t + \Delta t)\) as an integral from \(a(t + \Delta t)\) to \(b(t + \Delta t)\text{,}\) and split it into three integrals: one over the fixed interval \([a(t), b(t)]\text{,}\) and one over each of the two small intervals near the end points. These correspond to the three circled regions in Figure 3.5. For small \(\Delta t\text{,}\) the two small intervals are narrow, so each of their integrals can be approximated by the area of a single rectangle.
    🔗
    A fully rigorous proof is not required here. It is fine to use approximations such as these, provided you explain why they become exact as \(\Delta t \to 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Can you now connect the form of the rule in (3.6.1) to the Reynolds’ Transport Theorem in (3.1.2)?
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Derivation of governing equations.

    This is a re-derivation of what is already presented in the notes, giving you valuable practice to understand the concepts.
    🔗
    Starting from conservation of mass and momentum for a material volume moving in an inviscid fluid, use Reynolds’ Transport Theorem (3.1.2) to derive the equations:
    \begin{gather} \pd{\rho}{t} + \nabla \cdot(\rho \bu) = 0, \tag{3.6.2}\\ \DD{\bu}{t} = -\frac{1}{\rho} \nabla p + \bg. \tag{3.6.3} \end{gather}
    🔗
    What additional assumptions are required to transform the set of equations to the Euler equations of Definition 3.4.5? Apply these assumptions and conclude with the Euler equations.
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Pressure field in uniform flow.

    An inviscid, incompressible fluid of constant density \(\rho\text{,}\) in a gravitational field with acceleration vector \(\bg = -g \bk\) (with \(\bk\) being the unit vector in the positive \(z\)-direction) has uniform velocity
    \begin{equation*} \bu = (a + b t, \; t^3, \; \e^t). \end{equation*}
    The pressure at the origin is fixed at \(p_0\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Using the Euler equations in Definition 3.4.5, find the pressure field in terms of \(x, y, z, t, a, b, g, \rho\) and \(p_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    What can you say about the surfaces of constant pressure? Sketch this at a fixed time \(t\text{,}\) and hence describe the pressure field.

    6. Using streamlines: The clepsydra.

    One of the earliest means for measuring the passage of time, invented by the ancient Egyptians, was the clepsydra (or ‘water thief’): a large jar with a hole in its base was filled with water. The shape of the jar was such that the interval of time taken for the water surface to pass two equally-spaced markers on the side of the jar is constant.
    🔗
    In this question you will determine the shape of jar required to achieve this. In particular, we denote the radius of the (axisymmetric) jar at height \(z\) above the hole by \(r = a(z)\text{.}\) Note that, in general, the radius of the hole, \(a_h\text{,}\) differs from \(a(0)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Explain why the curve \(r = a(z)\) is a streamline.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    If the surface of the water lies at \(z = h(t)\text{,}\) use an appropriate form of Bernoulli’s principle to calculate the speed of liquid, \(u\text{,}\) leaving the jar at \(z = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    [You may assume that the desired surface speed \(u_S = |\dot{h}| \ll u\text{,}\) so that the flow is approximately steady and that the fluid pressure is atmospheric at \(z = 0\text{.}\)]
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Use the principle of conservation of mass to link \(u_S\text{,}\) \(u\text{,}\) \(a_h\) and \(a(h(t))\text{;}\) thereby determine the correct shape for a clepsydra.
    🔗

    8. Measuring airspeed with a Pitot tube.

    Aside: New in 2026.

    This exercise is a new addition to the notes in 2026. It is optional and not part of the homework.
    🔗
    Aircraft measure their airspeed using a Pitot tube: a thin tube that points forwards into the oncoming air, with its open tip facing the flow. The pressure inside the tube is compared with the pressure of the surrounding air. In the simplest version of the device, shown below, this comparison is made with a U-tube of water (a manometer). One arm of the U-tube is connected to the Pitot tube, and the other arm is open to the surrounding air.
    🔗
    Watch this short video demonstration from Cornell University.
    🔗
    Suppose that air of constant density \(\rho_\text{air}\) approaches the tip of the tube with uniform speed \(U\) and pressure \(p_\infty\text{.}\) The flow is steady, and the air entering the tube pushes the water down in the arm connected to the tube and up in the open arm. Measured from the same reference level, let the water surface be at height \(h_1\) in the arm connected to the tube and at height \(h_2\) in the open arm, so that the height difference is \(\Delta h = h_2 - h_1 \gt 0\text{.}\) The water has density \(\rho_w\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Explain why the air is brought to rest at the open tip of the tube. Hence, use an appropriate form of Bernoulli’s equation to show that the pressure at the tip is
    \begin{equation*} p_0 = p_\infty + \frac12 \rho_\text{air} U^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hint.
    Follow the streamline that ends at the tip of the tube, where the air is brought to rest. Then treat the water in the U-tube as a fluid at rest, as in Example 3.5.12.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    The air inside the tube is at rest, so the pressure \(p_0\) is transmitted to the water in one arm of the U-tube. The other arm is open to air at pressure \(p_\infty\text{.}\) Neglecting the weight of the air in the tubes, show that
    \begin{equation*} p_0 - p_\infty = \rho_w g \Delta h. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Deduce that
    \begin{equation*} U = \sqrt{\frac{2 \rho_w g \Delta h}{\rho_\text{air}}}. \end{equation*}
    Evaluate \(U\) when the water levels are at \(h_1 = 0.25\) m and \(h_2 = 0.75\) m, taking \(\rho_\text{air} = 1\,\text{kg/m}^3\text{,}\) \(\rho_w = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3\text{,}\) and \(g = 10\,\text{m/s}^2\text{.}\)
